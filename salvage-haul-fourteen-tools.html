Fourteen Tools,
Fourteen Dollars

I went looking for a side table. This is what came home instead.

01 / The Assignment

I had one thing on the list: a small table.

Nothing fancy. Just something I could restore and set next to my favorite reading spot on our front porch — big enough for a drink, my phone, maybe a book face-down when I get up to do something else. That was the whole assignment.

I struck out completely on the table.

I came home with fourteen tools instead.

02 / The Shops

Second Use and Earthwise, on the usual loop.

I make a loop through Second Use and Earthwise Architectural Salvage every so often, and honestly the table was only half the reason. The other half is that I find these places generate ideas. You walk past a stack of old growth fir, a bin of doorknobs, a wall of windows nobody makes anymore, and you leave with three projects you weren’t planning on. Sometimes I get lucky and find old tools worth adding to the collection. Sometimes I just get the ideas. Either way it’s a good hour.

This trip, I got both — just not the table.

The bin at Second Use

Two Perfect Handle screwdrivers, sitting in a bin. Two dollars each.

I have a soft spot for Perfect Handles. That is probably underselling it — it’s closer to a minor obsession. The pattern is a full flat steel tang running the entire length of the tool with wood scales riveted to either side, so the steel is exposed all the way around the perimeter of the handle. The design is credited to H.D. Smith & Co. and got copied by half the industry, which is why you find them everywhere and why they’re still worth picking up when you do.

One of mine is stamped IRWIN on the shank. The stamp is faint enough that I didn’t see it in person — I only noticed it once I got home. More on that in a minute.

For four dollars, I’d have taken them in worse shape than this.

The tub at Earthwise

The rest of the pile was outside.

Not on a shelf, not in the shop — outside, in a small plastic tub, exposed to the weather and sitting in a couple inches of rainwater. No price on the tub, no tag on anything in it. Twelve tools, give or take, marinating.

I asked the clerk if he’d take ten dollars for the lot.

He said yes. Immediately. Without so much as a pause to think it over.

And of course the second the words left his mouth I thought, well, I should have said five. Ha! That’s the salvage yard tax on being a slightly-too-polite negotiator. Ten dollars for twelve tools is still ten dollars for twelve tools, so I’m not going to pretend I’m wounded here.

03 / What Actually Came Home

Fourteen pieces, all in.

One Estwing 16 oz curved claw hammer with the stacked leather grip. One metal-bodied soft-face hammer with replaceable faces on a wood handle. Five pliers and cutters — a bare forged pair with no grips at all, two diagonal cutters, an end nipper, and a small red-handled pair. Five wood-handled screwdrivers with steel ferrules. And the two Perfect Handles from Second Use.

The Estwing is the standout, and it’s the one I spent the most time on.

What the butt plate says

The butt plate reads ESTWING, then ROCKFORD·ILL., then MADE IN U.S.A., then 16 OZ HEAD. I put it on a scale and it is, in fact, sixteen ounces — which is a small thing, but I like when the tool tells the truth about itself.

The dating clue is what’s not on the plate. It says ROCKFORD·ILL. with no ZIP code. Estwing’s later tools are marked Rockford, IL 61109. ZIP codes came into use in 1963, and the spelled-out ILL. belongs to the same older convention. So this hammer is very likely older than the early sixties. I’m not going to try to pin it tighter than that, and I’ll explain below why I’m being careful about it.

There’s also a magenta paint swipe across the plate, which I’m confident is a shop lot mark and not anything Estwing did.

04 / The Part Where I Asked the Robots

Same photo, three confident answers, forty years apart.

Here’s where the trip got more interesting than a trip to the salvage yard usually gets.

I photographed the whole pile and handed the same images to three different AI models — ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude — and asked each of them to identify the tools, with makers and ages if possible.

I got three confident answers. They did not agree.

ChatGPT was the most impressive-sounding of the three. It correctly called the Estwing, walked through the one-piece forged construction and the stacked leather grip, and then told me it could see a patent marking on the butt plate and read it as Pat. No. 16820. It layered on the history: filed in 1924, issued 1926, reissued December 1927. On the strength of that reading it revised its estimate from “probably mid-century” to “likely late 1920s to 1930s.”

“That patent number is not on my hammer.”

Claude went back to the original file at full resolution, cropped the plate, rotated it, and stretched the contrast until every stamped character was legible. ESTWING. ROCKFORD·ILL. MADE IN U.S.A. 16 OZ HEAD. That’s the whole inventory. Pat. 16820 is a real marking that shows up on some vintage Estwings, which is probably why it was sitting there to be reached for, but it isn’t on mine — and a decade of dating got built on top of it anyway.

Gemini went the other direction entirely and put the hammer at 1950s to 1970s based on the “lacquered varnish finish” on the grip, which is not really a thing you can determine from a photograph either.

So: same photo, two models, a forty year spread, both delivered in the same steady confident register.

To be fair, Gemini got something the other one flat missed. It recognized the two right-hand tools as Perfect Handle pattern and correctly credited H.D. Smith with the original design. ChatGPT had filed those as “extra-long screwdrivers, maker unknown.” That’s a real call and Gemini deserves the credit for it.

But both of them handed me lists of manufacturers — Channellock, Klein, Utica, Crescent, Xcelite, Vaco, Thor, Garland, Mac, Goodell-Pratt — for tools with no visible markings whatsoever. Those aren’t identifications. They’re period-plausible brand names arranged in the grammar of an identification, which is a very different thing and much harder to notice.

And here’s the part I keep turning over. Across all fourteen tools, exactly one maker’s mark turned out to be genuinely legible — that IRWIN stamp on the screwdriver shank. None of the three models read it off the photographs. I found it at home, with the tool in my hand and the light coming in low across the steel.

“The one piece of hard evidence in the entire pile came from the object, not from the analysis.”

I don’t think the lesson here is “AI is useless for this.” It clearly isn’t — I know more about that hammer than I did on Saturday. The lesson is that the confidence doesn’t scale with the evidence. All three answers sounded equally sure. Only one of them was checkable, and only because I asked a follow-up question instead of taking the first answer home.

Which, come to think of it, is roughly how you should shop a salvage yard too.

05 / What Happens Next

Everything except the two hammers is in the Evapo-Rust right now.

I’ll give it about 24 hours and let the magic juice do its thing.

The hammers are staying out of the bath deliberately. Evapo-Rust is selective — it goes after iron oxide and leaves the base steel alone — so it isn’t going to eat a stamp. But the Estwing’s patina is doing real work as evidence right now, and the painted yellow and black bands on that leather grip are exactly the kind of thing that a solvent lifts first and you never get back.

After the soak, the plan is the Bucktool 1x30 belt sander to knock things back, then sandpaper and steel wool by hand to bring them the rest of the way. WD-40 will show up somewhere in there. Paste wax at the end to keep the freshly cleaned steel from going right back to rust, because this is Seattle and the metal knows it.

One Note to My Future Self

Photograph every shank and ferrule between the Evapo-Rust and the belt sander. The rust is what’s hiding the faint stamps right now, and the belt is what will erase them permanently. There’s a window in between where a maker’s mark is the most readable it will ever be, and it’s about ten minutes wide.

06 / What I’m Keeping

Not all of it, and that’s fine.

A pile like this is partly for me and partly for the people around me. Whatever I don’t keep will go to friends and family who want them, and I’ll probably list a couple.

The hammers I’m keeping. The two Perfect Handles I’m keeping if they clean up the way I think they will — see previously noted minor obsession. And that bare forged pair of pliers with no grips on them is the sleeper of the whole lot. They look like they might be hand forged, and if they clean up into good users, they’re staying too.

A restoration entry on this lot is coming once the work is done. Table’s still on the list.
