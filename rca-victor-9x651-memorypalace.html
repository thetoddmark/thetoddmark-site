The radio that became a computer

01 / The Object

A 1950 RCA Victor 9-X-651. Brown Bakelite cabinet, three knobs, AM and shortwave bands.

Serial number B 003994. Roughly the size of a shoebox. Built by the Radio Corporation of America at the height of their postwar consumer electronics dominance, when RCA was the kind of company that made you feel like the future was already here.

It came to me via Spade and Archer — a Portland prop house and antique dealer — with their sticker still on the bottom. Before that: unknown. Seventy-five years of kitchens, living rooms, garages. Whoever owned it last put felt pads on the feet and kept it around long enough that someone thought it was worth selling.

I thought it was worth something too. Just not what it used to be.

02 / The Story

The 9-X-651 is one of the most common Bakelite radios ever produced. Collectors will tell you that with a slight wince.

It means low desirability, modest value, abundant supply. You can find them at every estate sale in America.

I find that comforting rather than discouraging. It means this radio was in a lot of homes. It meant something to a lot of people. The shortwave band alone tells you something — in 1950, being able to pull in a signal from across the world felt like a miracle. This little brown box sat on a counter somewhere and made the world feel smaller.

It doesn’t do that anymore. The capacitors are shot, the tubes are old, and I’m not an electronics restorer. What I am is someone who thinks old objects deserve a second life that matches what they actually are — not a museum piece, not a prop, not a conversation starter that sits gathering dust.

“Not a museum piece, not a prop, not a conversation starter that sits gathering dust. So I gave it a job.”

So I gave it a job.

03 / The Process

Not restoration. Transformation.

I had a reference image in mind before I started — a period-correct RCA Victor in a similar cabinet with ivory grille slats and brass knobs. Not original. Not historically accurate. But a considered, intentional aesthetic that honors the object’s design language while making it clearly something new.

That’s the line I try to walk.

Disassembly

Chassis out, knobs pulled, back panel off. The Bakelite cabinet is what I was after — the electronics were never coming back. Photographed everything before touching it: the grimy front face, the original label on the bottom, the Spade and Archer sticker. Those before shots matter.

Bakelite Cleaning and Polish

The cabinet was oxidized, hazy, and carrying decades of grime in every groove. The process here is straightforward but requires patience:

  • Wash first. Warm water, mild dish soap, soft cloth. Get the surface grime off before you do anything else. Let it dry completely.
  • Polish. Flitz — a metal and plastic polish that’s gentle enough for Bakelite’s thin surface layer. Applied with a soft cloth, worked in sections, buffed off. The brown residue that comes off on the rag is tobacco tar and oxidation. It’s satisfying in a gross way.
  • Protect. Renaissance Wax as the final step on anything I want to stay polished without attracting dust. Thin coat, buff to a shine. Done.

The Bakelite came back. Deep chocolate brown, light catching the chamfered edges. Worth the time.

Grille Slats — Heirloom White

This is the transformation step. The original grille slats are the same dark brown as the rest of the cabinet. The reference aesthetic called for ivory — warm, not stark white — against that dark Bakelite.

Blue painter’s tape over every surface except the slat faces — pressed firmly into edges, especially around the knob openings at the bottom. Rust-Oleum Painter’s Touch 2X in Heirloom White, Satin. Satin because the polished Bakelite body has a natural semi-gloss — flat paint would look chalky against it, gloss would look cheap. Satin reads as intentional.

Three coats: a dust coat first, then two light coats with 10 minutes between each. Tape pulled at 25 minutes while still slightly tacky — pulling fully cured paint tears the edge. Some bleed into the gaps between slats. Toothpick along the gap floors once fully cured, then a cotton swab with mineral spirits for stubborn spots. The Flitz pass on the Bakelite body handled the rest.

Knobs

The original brown Bakelite knobs were replaced with gold aluminum knurled knobs — the kind you’d find on audio equipment or guitar amplifiers. Three of them, same size, set-screw fit over the original D-shafts.

The effect is exactly right. Warm gold against ivory against dark chocolate. It reads as mid-century without pretending to be something it isn’t.

The New Life — MemoryPalace

The chassis that came out was replaced with something different: my MemoryPalace NAS — a Raspberry Pi 5 with a 20TB hard drive and 500GB SSD, running as my home Time Machine backup system.

The radio that once pulled shortwave signals from across the world now quietly backs up every photo, document, and project file on the network. It runs a FastAPI dashboard and sends alerts via ntfy when something needs attention.

The dial face — original RCA Victor graphics, AM and shortwave bands still legible — sits behind the original glass. The knobs don’t do anything. The whole thing is a shell around a small computer doing useful work.

“The radio that once pulled shortwave signals from across the world now quietly backs up every photo on the network.”

I like that. A lot.

05 / The Outcome

Seventy-five years old. Bakelite shell. Silicon heart. Still doing its job.

The 9-X-651 is sitting on a shelf in my home office. It looks like what it is: an old radio that got a second opinion.

The Bakelite is deep and glossy. The slats are clean. The knobs catch the light. Behind the glass, the original dial still says RCA Victor, still shows 55 through 160 on the AM band and the shortwave frequencies nobody tunes to anymore.

Nobody has to know there’s a Raspberry Pi inside. But I know.

The Honest Take

The Bakelite cleaning is the most satisfying part of a project like this — the transformation from dull, grime-covered plastic to something with actual depth and warmth is immediate and dramatic. Flitz does the work. Patience does the rest.

The grille slat paint is the detail that makes this. Satin Heirloom White against polished dark Bakelite is a combination that looks considered without looking precious. The tape work and cleanup add time but the edge quality is the difference between something that looks intentional and something that looks like a project.

The Raspberry Pi installation is the part people ask about. The short answer: it fits, it runs cool, and it’s been solid. The longer answer is its own write-up.
