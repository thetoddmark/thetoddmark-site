Restoration No. 12

RCA Victor
9-X-651

The radio that became the Memory Palace

01 / The Object

A tabletop radio from 1950. It used to pull in broadcasts from across the continent.

The RCA Victor 9-X-651 is a five-tube AM superheterodyne table radio produced in the early 1950s. It's a small set by the standards of the era — compact enough to sit on a nightstand or kitchen shelf, big enough that the speaker actually has something to say. The cabinet is dark phenolic plastic, molded into that rounded postwar shape that looks halfway between Streamline Moderne and a loaf of bread. The dial is a half-moon of amber and ivory, lit from behind, with the station frequencies arranged around the arc in that particular mid-century font that somehow still feels current.

RCA knew what they were doing in 1950. The brand was at its peak — they made the televisions, the record players, the radios. The 9-X-651 wasn't their flagship model. It was the one that sold to everyone else. The one that ended up in the kitchen. The one that played the game on Sunday and the news at six. The one that got handed down and eventually forgotten in a box in someone's garage, waiting.

02 / The Find

It had been painted. Someone, at some point, decided that was a good idea.

The cabinet had been oversprayed — not badly, not dramatically, but enough that you could tell immediately something was off. The original phenolic had a depth to it, a warmth in the dark plastic that overspray flattens into something dull and uneven. From a distance the radio looked fine. Up close, you could see the texture was wrong, the color too uniform, the surface too matte where it should have had that particular mid-century sheen.

Inside was the more interesting story. The original tube chassis was intact — all five positions occupied — but the capacitors were the kind that made you want to replace them on principle alone. Vintage paper and electrolytic caps don't age gracefully. They dry out, shift value, fail in ways that can take a speaker with them. The kind of thing that works fine until it absolutely doesn't.

"The cabinet had been painted. The electronics had been left alone. Someone cared about how it looked but not what it did."

The dial was the saving grace. It had survived the overspray largely intact — the original escutcheon still crisp, the frequency markings legible, the amber tint of the lens still doing its job. In a restoration like this, that matters. The dial is the face of the radio. If it's gone, it's a different project entirely.

03 / The Process

Cabinet first. Then the guts. In that order, for good reason.

The overspray came off with patience and the right solvent. The original phenolic underneath was in solid shape — the surface had been protected by whatever they'd put over it, which is one of the few things in this project's favor. Working methodically around the cabinet, taking care around the dial escutcheon and the speaker grille cloth, the original finish came back.

Then the interior.

The old components came out. All of them. When you're dealing with a radio this age, doing a partial recap is a gamble — you fix what you can see and leave problems waiting. The right call is to go all the way: clear the chassis, document what was there, and rebuild with modern equivalents that will actually hold their values for another generation.

04 / After Photos

The dial glows the way it should. The speaker sounds the way it should.

05 / The Outcome

Seventy-five years old. Still pulling stations. Still worth listening to.

The 9-X-651 is sitting in the house now. It's not hidden away as a display piece — it's plugged in, the dial lit, the speaker doing what speakers are for. When I turn it on, it warms up in that tube-radio way where the sound comes in gradually, which is either charming or inconvenient depending on how patient you are. I find it charming.

I called it the Memory Palace because that's what these radios are. Every station it can pull in was also being pulled in somewhere in 1950 — the same frequencies, different broadcasts, different listeners. There's something worth thinking about in that. Not in a precious way. Just in the way that good objects make you consider the distance between then and now.

The Honest Take

The cabinet work was the part that required the most care. The overspray removal needed patience and the right approach — too aggressive and you risk the original surface, too timid and you don't actually fix it. Taking it slow and working in sections paid off. The original phenolic underneath was worth recovering.

Going all the way on the electronics was the right call. A partial recap on a radio this age is a deferred problem. The new components will hold spec for decades. The tubes test well and they're staying — they're part of what makes this thing sound the way it sounds. A solid-state swap would have been faster, but that's a different radio.

06 / Supplies Used

What I Used

As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases. Some links below are affiliate links — I earn a small commission if you buy through them, at no extra cost to you. I only list what I actually used.

  • DeoxIT D5 Contact Cleaner For the tube sockets and switch contacts. Apply, work it in, wipe back. Standard first step on any vintage electronic restoration.
  • Electrolytic Capacitors (to spec) Full recap — all original electrolytics replaced with modern equivalents matched to the original values. Not a shortcut, but the right call on a radio this age.
  • Film Capacitors (coupling and bypass) Original paper caps replaced with modern polyester film equivalents. Better stability, no moisture absorption, will outlast the chassis.
  • Novus Plastic Polish For the phenolic cabinet after the overspray was removed. Works on most vintage plastics without hazing — this is my go-to for Bakelite and similar materials.
  • Isopropyl Alcohol (90%+) For cleaning the chassis and components before work started. Get the highest percentage you can find — anything below 90% has too much water content for electronics work.
  • Renaissance Wax Thin coat over the cabinet as a final seal. Protects the restored phenolic surface without looking artificial. Same product, same result as on every other piece I've used it on.
  • Solder + Rosin Flux 60/40 for the component work. Fresh solder on old joints makes a measurable difference — the cold joints on a radio this age are worth reflowing even where they're not failing yet.
