Restoration No. 11

The HOMART
Brass Flashlight

A 1930s Sears relic that still throws light

01 / The Object

Built to be used, not displayed. Though right now, it's doing both.

This is a HOMART brass flashlight, a Sears house brand sold through their catalog and retail stores in the early-to-mid 1930s. It's stamped PAT. 1,774,722 - a patent granted September 2, 1930 to Wesley J. Officer of Blake Manufacturing Co. - and MADE IN U.S.A. on the side-mounted chrome switch plate.

The body is solid brass, heavily ribbed for grip, with the HOMART name cast in raised block letters along the barrel. The head flares out in that unmistakable Art Deco bulb shape, finished with triple rings on the tail cap. The side switch housing is a small piece of sculpture on its own - aerodynamic, chrome-plated, riveted in place. It looks like something off a 1930s locomotive.

Roughly 90 years old. And it works.

02 / The Story

It needed work. That's why I bought it.

I picked this one up knowing it needed work. Found it at Goodwill for $21 - probably overpaid, but I knew what I was getting into. The brass had gone dull and greenish, the switch was stiff, and when I dropped batteries in - nothing. Dead.

That's not unusual for a flashlight this old. What was a little surprising was figuring out why it wasn't working. More on that in a moment.

What drew me to the HOMART in the first place was the form. This thing was clearly designed by someone who gave a damn. The ribbing, the raised lettering, the Art Deco switch - this isn't a utilitarian object that happened to look good. Someone made intentional choices here. In an era before injection-molded plastic, flashlights like this were built to last a generation. This one lasted three.

"In an era before injection-molded plastic, flashlights like this were built to last a generation. This one lasted three."

03 / The Process

Polish. Fix the circuit. Drop in the LED. Seal it.

Polish First

No electrolysis on this one - the brass didn't need it. What it needed was elbow grease.

I worked the entire body with Flitz polish, blue shop rags, and a lot of Q-tips for the ribbing and crevices around the switch housing. The ribbing is the most tedious part - there are dozens of tight grooves that trap oxidation, and you have to work each one. Q-tips are the only tool that gets in there cleanly.

Multiple passes. The transformation from dull olive-green to warm brass took longer than expected but the result speaks for itself.

The Electrical Problem

Here's where it got interesting.

When I finally got the flashlight cleaned up and dropped in batteries - still nothing. I suspected the contacts, which is common on vintage flashlights. What I didn't fully appreciate going in is that on a flashlight like this, I'm not positive but I think the entire brass body functions as part of the electrical circuit - it serves as the ground path. Every contact point matters: the battery terminals, the bulb socket, the switch.

Ninety years of oxidation had effectively broken the circuit at multiple points.

The fix:

  • DeoxIT D5 on the switch contacts and bulb socket - applied, worked in, wiped back
  • 400-grit sandpaper on the battery contact points and bulb base - light passes to break through the oxidation layer and restore bare metal contact

That combination did the trick. The moment it clicked on was genuinely satisfying.

LED Upgrade

The original bulb was long gone. Rather than hunt for a period-correct replacement, I installed a Draco Products Cree PR2 LED drop-in. It's a direct swap for the original PR2 bulb format - no modification needed, runs on standard D-cell batteries, and throws a clean bright beam.

Purists might object. I'd argue a flashlight that works is more faithful to its original purpose than one sitting dark in a display case.

Seal and Protect

Final step: a coat of Renaissance Wax over the entire brass body. It locks in the polish, slows future oxidation, and leaves a finish that's protective without looking artificial. This is my standard final step on brass and it works.

05 / The Outcome

90 years old. Brass body. LED heart. Still throwing light.

The HOMART is sitting on my desk in my home office right now. I like how it looks - the warm brass, the chrome switch, the Art Deco lines. It's a good object to have around.

Will I keep it forever? Probably not. At some point it'll go to make room for the next project. But for now it's earning its spot.

The Honest Take

The electrical diagnosis was the interesting part of this one. Polishing brass is satisfying but it's not complicated - multiple passes with Flitz and Q-tips, patience with the ribbing, done. The circuit problem was more interesting to solve. DeoxIT and 400-grit sandpaper at the contact points is a combination I'll use again on vintage electrical restorations.

The LED swap is a practical call I'm comfortable with. The Draco Products Cree PR2 is a drop-in - no drilling, no modification, no damage to the original form. It runs on the same batteries the flashlight was designed for and throws a better beam than the original incandescent ever did. The purpose of a flashlight is to make light. This one makes light.
